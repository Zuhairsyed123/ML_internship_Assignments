00 — Abstract

Ranking pages by decline risk, checked against its own baseline

Manual review cannot scale to a content catalog of tens of thousands of pages, so this work asks whether observable search and engagement signals can rank pages by decline risk well enough to drive a review queue. A rule-based baseline and three trained models — Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, and Random Forest — were built on 30,000 content pages from FlyRank's starter release and evaluated on a client-holdout split using Precision@50 and Precision@100. On that split, Random Forest reached a Precision@50 of 0.68 against a baseline of 0.24, a roughly 2.8× lift, with impression volume and position among the strongest signals. An earlier run of the same modeling code on a different split produced the opposite ranking — baseline ahead of every trained model — and that disagreement is reported here rather than hidden. The output is a four-tier action queue (monitor, refresh, refresh-and-review-CTR, expand-and-refresh) meant to route human review, not to publish or act on its own.

01 — Introduction

Why manual review doesn't scale

A content or SEO team can read a handful of pages closely in a day. A production catalog runs to tens of thousands of pages, each with its own trajectory of impressions, clicks, position, and engagement. Somewhere in that catalog is a small set of pages worth a human's time this week — pages that are visible, losing ground, and fixable. The question this project takes on is whether that set can be found by ranking pages on signals that are already being logged, rather than by a team paging through spreadsheets or a fixed if-this-then-that rule.

The decision this work supports is narrow and deliberate: which pages a content team should look at first, out of a catalog too large to review by hand. It is not a decision about what to change on any given page, and it is not a claim about why a page is declining — only about where attention is most likely to be worth spending.

02 — Dataset

What this is built on

The starter release used for this paper is a 30,000-row, 44-column anonymized extract (content_refresh_anonymized.csv) drawn from FlyRank's internship warehouse. A parallel path through the full Hugging Face warehouse release (fact_content_daily_performance, month=2026-03) was also explored for feature construction and leakage testing, restricted to pages with at least 100 monthly impressions.

Grain

One row is one content page. The label used throughout, is_declining_label, is defined as trend_direction == "down" in the starter set — a proxy for decline, not a directly measured business outcome.

03 — Methodology

Label, features, baseline, and how the split was chosen

Target

is_declining_label = trend_direction == "down". This is a defined proxy rather than an observed business outcome; a stronger, future-window target (for example, a 30-day-forward traffic drop measured directly from the warehouse) was identified as a future improvement but not substituted here.

Features

Thirteen features fed the trained models: log-transformed 90-day impressions, clicks, pageviews, and sessions; average search position; CTR; engagement rate; scroll rate; word count; search volume; competition score; days since last update; and a binary striking-distance flag (average position 4–10).

Baseline

The hand-coded baseline scores each page as is_striking × (0.5 × impression percentile + 0.5 × (1 − CTR percentile)), carrying the reason code low_ctr_striking_distance and the action optimize_metadata_and_refresh. It only ever flags pages already in striking distance (position 4–10); everything else scores zero.

Models

Logistic Regression, a Decision Tree, and a Random Forest were compared against the baseline, all trained on the same thirteen features.

Validation design

Splits were grouped by client_id using GroupShuffleSplit, holding out entire clients rather than random rows, specifically to stop a model from learning client-specific baselines instead of a pattern that generalizes.

Leakage prevention

A separate audit on the warehouse data deliberately built a "leaked" version of the model that included imp_future — the same future-impressions window used to construct the label — as a feature. That leaked model reached 97.1% accuracy and 97.6% precision under a random split, with imp_future alone accounting for 42.7% of its feature importance. The honest model, with that column removed, scored 64.1% accuracy and 65.2% precision on the same random split, falling to 57.3% accuracy and 60.9% precision once the split was regrouped by client — a reminder that both the leak and the split matter.

04 — Results

The model comparison — and a discrepancy worth naming

On the client-holdout split used for the final action queue, Random Forest was the strongest model on every metric:

Model ROC AUC Avg. Precision Precision@50 Precision@100
Baseline (rule-based) 0.627 0.468 0.24 0.36
Logistic Regression 0.700 0.522 0.40 0.44
Decision Tree 0.742 0.575 0.62 0.60
Random Forest 0.747 0.610 0.68 0.70

At Precision@50, Random Forest delivers roughly a 2.8× lift over the baseline (34 of the top 50 flagged pages are observed to decline, versus 12 for the baseline); at Precision@100 the lift narrows to roughly 1.9×. Reported signal strength, in order: overall search demand (days_with_impressions, log-impressions) contributed most, followed by position and CTR, followed by content age and days since last update.

Reported honestly

An earlier run of the identical baseline-vs-model code, on the same starter dataset but a different 80/20 client split, produced the opposite result: the baseline scored a Precision@50 of 0.74, ahead of Logistic Regression (0.68) and Random Forest (0.70). That run is not discarded here — it's a genuine inconsistency between two executions of this project, and it means the "Random Forest beats the baseline" finding above should be read as a result of this particular split, not as a settled fact independent of how the data was divided.

05 — Limitations

What this cannot claim

  1. Split-dependent result. As shown in Results, re-running the same models on a different split of the same data flipped the ranking — baseline ahead of every trained model. The headline finding here is a property of this evaluation, not an invariant one.
  2. Proxy label, not a measured outcome. is_declining_label is derived from a categorical trend field, not from a directly observed traffic drop over a defined future window.
  3. Observational, not causal. Position, CTR, staleness, and word count are measured together with decline, not manipulated. This project cannot prove that refreshing a page causes traffic to recover, and it cannot predict or explain Google's ranking algorithm.
  4. Counterintuitive signal, unexplained. Long-form content declined more than short content in this dataset. That is reported as an observed pattern, not a recommendation to shorten pages — the underlying cause (competition, topic type, publication era) was not isolated.
  5. Single-snapshot scope. The starter dataset is a fixed extract; seasonal effects and any changes in FlyRank's product flags since extraction are not captured.
06 — Recommendations

The action playbook

Every page in the queue routes to one of five actions, ordered here from lowest to highest urgency, each intended for human review rather than automatic execution:

  • Monitor — largest share of the queue (43.6%); no action needed yet, re-check next cycle.
  • Refresh — standard update: refresh statistics, images, and republish with a new timestamp.
  • Refresh & review CTR — run a title/meta-description test and check search-intent match; the single largest "do something now" segment (22.2%).
  • Refresh & review engagement — improve internal linking and on-page engagement elements for pages with weak scroll or engagement rate.
  • Expand & protect — the smallest, highest-value slice (0.3%): deepen content, cover missing subtopics, and protect existing pillar pages from decline.

Nothing here is meant to run unattended: no automatic title/meta rewriting, no automatic publishing, and no bulk action without a human confirming the reason code first.
